Grounding skills you can use between sessions

Four simple techniques to steady your body when a memory or a hard day pulls you off balance.

By Jess Rebelo, LCSW · October 14, 2026

Photo: hands around a mug, window light

Therapy happens in a session, but most of life happens between them. When a reminder of the past lands hard, your body can react before your mind catches up: a racing heart, shallow breath, a sense of being far away. Grounding brings your attention back to the present, where you are safe enough to think clearly.

These skills do not erase what happened. They give you a way to steady yourself so you can get through the moment.

1. Name what is around you

Slowly notice five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell and one you can taste. Say them out loud if you can. Naming forces your attention outward, into the room you are actually in.

2. Lengthen the exhale

Breathe in through your nose for a count of four, then out for a count of six. A longer exhale signals your nervous system to slow down. Five rounds is enough to notice a change.

The goal is not to feel calm. It is to feel here.

3. Orient to the room

Turn your head slowly and let your eyes rest on ordinary objects. Then tell yourself three facts: where you are, today’s date, and how old you are. Memories feel present; facts remind you they are past.

4. Feel the support under you

Press your feet into the floor. Notice the chair or bed holding your weight. Let your shoulders drop as you exhale. Your body learns safety through sensation as much as through thought.

When to reach out

Practice these when you feel fine, so they are familiar when you need them. If they stop helping, or you find yourself avoiding more of your life, that is a good time to talk with a clinician.

If you are in crisis

Call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or call 911. This article is educational and not a substitute for care.
